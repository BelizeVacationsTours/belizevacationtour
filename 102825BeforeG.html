Cockscomb Basin Wildlife Sanctuary

The Cockscomb Basin Wildlife Sanctuary is the world's first jaguar preserve in Belize, protecting over 128,000 acres of rainforest. It offers visitors hiking trails, river tubing, and waterfall visits, and is home to jaguars, tapirs, and hundreds of bird species. While the sanctuary is a jaguar hotspot, seeing one is unlikely due to their elusive nature.

What the Preserve Offers

  • Wildlife: A habitat for an estimated 80 jaguars, tapirs, peccaries, and coatis. It has over 290 recorded bird species, including Scarlet Macaws and Keel-billed Toucans.
  • Activities: Explore numerous well-marked hiking trails (easy to challenging), go tubing down the river, or hike to waterfalls. Bird watching is a popular activity.
  • Plant life: Home to diverse plant species, including orchids, ferns, and hardwoods like mahogany and Ceiba trees.

Planning Your Visit

  • Location: Accessed from the Southern Highway, about 20 miles south of Dangriga.
  • Amenities: A visitor center and a gift shop are near the entrance. Rustic cabins are available, but it is best to bring your own food.
  • What to bring: Pack sturdy shoes, insect repellent, sunscreen, and plenty of water.
  • Regulations: Stay on marked trails, no pets, and do not use soaps in the rivers.

Traditional Mayan Chocolate Making

Chocolate making in Belize involves a traditional process that begins with organic cacao beans, which are harvested, fermented, and sun-dried. Guests can participate in hands-on experiences, roasting the beans on a clay griddle, grinding them on a stone grinder (metate), and then creating their own chocolate by adding sugar and other ingredients. This is often presented as an educational and cultural tour focusing on Mayan traditions.

The Traditional Process

  • Harvesting & Fermenting: Cacao pods are harvested, cracked open, and the beans are fermented to develop flavor.
  • Drying & Roasting: Beans are sun-dried and then roasted by participants on a traditional clay griddle (comal).
  • Shelling & Grinding: The shells are separated, and the inner cacao nibs are ground into a paste on a stone metate.
  • Finishing: Sugar and other ingredients are added to the paste, which is then shaped into chocolate bars for you to sample!

This hands-on cultural experience is often found in Maya villages like Maya Center or in the Toledo district.
